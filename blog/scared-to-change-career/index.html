Scared to change career? Start by making the worry specific

Joe Markland, Career Coach

You've probably got a rough idea of what you'd move to. You might have looked at courses, saved a few job adverts, even drafted the email to someone who does the work you're curious about. And then you've stopped. You haven't decided against it. Every time you get close, something in your stomach tells you to leave it for now.

If that sounds familiar, you probably know plenty about the move already. What's slowing you down is fear, which is a reasonable response to a decision with real consequences, and it's worth taking seriously rather than trying to talk yourself out of.

This post is about what that fear might be doing, why it tends to stick around, and how to look at it in a way that helps you decide, whichever way you go.

Why the fear doesn't go away on its own

A lot of career advice treats fear as something to push through. Feel the fear, do it anyway. That works for some people. For plenty of others, it just adds a second problem: now you're scared and you feel bad about being scared.

Psychologists who study stress describe two quick judgements we make about anything that feels threatening. First, how much could this hurt me? Second, could I cope if it did? When a career change feels big and the answer to the second question is a vague "I don't know", the whole thing tends to feel bigger than it is.

That's often where people get stuck. The worry may well be fair. It's usually too blurry to do anything with, though.

Relief now, cost later

There's another reason the fear sticks. Every time you step back from the move, you get a small reward: relief. You close the job advert and feel lighter. You decide to wait until after Christmas and the knot in your chest loosens.

That relief is immediate. The benefit of making the move is delayed and uncertain. So each time you avoid it, avoiding it gets a little easier, and the move gets a little harder to start. Research on avoidance describes this pattern, and it shows up in plenty of areas of life, not just careers.

None of this makes you weak or lazy. Most people respond to the rewards right in front of them.

One big fear, several smaller worries

When people say they're scared to change career, it usually turns out to be several worries bundled together and never looked at one by one. For example:

  • Money: whether you could manage a lower salary for a while, and for how long.
  • Other people: what your family, friends or current colleagues would think.
  • Starting again: being the new person, or the least experienced one in the room.
  • Getting it wrong: leaving something secure and finding the new thing isn't better.

Bundled together, they feel like one solid wall. Pulled apart, they become specific questions, and specific questions can be answered, planned for or tested.

Try writing each worry down on its own line. Then, next to each one, write the worst realistic outcome (not the worst imaginable one) and what you'd do if it happened. You'll often find that some worries shrink once they're written down. Others don't, and those are worth paying attention to.

Some fears are worth keeping

Fear isn't always something to get past. Sometimes it's pointing at something real. If your rent depends on your current salary, the worry about money is doing a useful job.

The useful question is which of your worries are telling you something you need to plan around, and which are just making the step feel bigger than it is. A safeguard might be saving three months' outgoings first, testing the new field with a short course, or talking to two people who've made a similar move. It might be deciding the timing is wrong for now.

Deciding not to change career, after looking at it properly, is a legitimate result. What tends to wear people down is the third option: not deciding, and carrying the question around for years.

A question to sit with

Here's one question worth some honest thought:

What would you do next if the downside felt half as significant as it does right now?

You don't have to act on the answer. But if the answer comes quickly and clearly, it tells you something about where the fear is sitting.

If you'd like to look at this more closely

Fear of the downside is one of several things that can get in the way of a career move you already know you want to make. Sometimes it's the main one. Sometimes something else is doing more of the work, like doubting you could do the new job, or simply not having the time or energy to start.
