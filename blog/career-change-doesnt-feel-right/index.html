Career change doesn't feel right? It might be wrong, or it might just be new

Joe Markland, Career Coach

You've thought it through. The new direction makes sense. Maybe you've had the conversations, looked at the roles, even been offered something. On paper, it adds up.

And yet it doesn't feel right. You can't quite picture yourself doing it, and every time you get close to committing, something pulls you back.

If that sounds familiar, this post won't tell you whether to make the move. It'll help you work out what that "doesn't feel right" feeling might be telling you, because it isn't always telling you the same thing.

The feeling usually gets treated as a verdict

A lot of what's written about this comes as lists of signs you're in the wrong career, or reasons people regret changing. Both treat the feeling as an answer. Either where you are is wrong, or where you're heading is.

Sometimes that's true. But "this doesn't feel like me" can come from a few different places, and each one needs a different response.

Three things that can sit underneath it

It's unfamiliar. You've had little or no experience of the new role, so there's no convincing picture of yourself in it yet. Anything you've never done will feel a bit like someone else's life. That doesn't make it wrong. It means you're short of evidence.

It's a real mismatch. The work, the environment or the values might not suit you. That's a perfectly good reason not to go ahead, and finding it out isn't a failure.

Something else is shaping it. Low confidence, worry about the risk, being short of time and energy, other people's expectations, or a difficult workplace can all make a move feel less like you than it otherwise would. The feeling is real. The cause just isn't about who you are.

From the inside, these three can feel exactly the same. That's what makes this kind of stuck so hard to read.

When the temporary job quietly becomes who you are

One version of this is common enough that I've given it a name: Placeholder Drift.

You took a job to tide you over. It was meant to be for a year. Then it was three, and somewhere along the way it became how you describe yourself to people. Now any move away from it feels like a stranger's career, even if it's the one you meant to have all along.

Placeholder Drift is my label for a recognisable situation, not a research-established condition. But if it sounds like you, it's worth knowing that some of the "not me" feeling may be the drift talking.

There's a mirror image, too: the move other people keep encouraging you to make that you can't picture yourself doing. Their enthusiasm is worth listening to, but the decision is still yours.

Why thinking harder rarely settles it

If the issue is unfamiliarity, more thinking won't fix it, because what's missing is experience. You can't imagine your way into knowing how a job feels on a wet Tuesday in February.

Herminia Ibarra's research (1999) describes professionals moving into new roles by trying out "provisional selves": testing versions of themselves in practice and judging them against their own experience and the feedback they got. A sense of who you are at work tends to build up through doing things.

That's useful, because it means you don't have to settle the question in your head before you're allowed to move.

A small test tells you more than a big decision

The question worth holding onto is this one:

Is this next step wrong for me, or just unfamiliar because I haven't tested it yet?

You answer it with a small experiment. A useful one has four parts:

  • A single question. What are you trying to find out? Not "should I change career?" but something like "do I enjoy running a group discussion?"
  • An action someone could see. What will you do, where and with whom?
  • A manageable scope. What can you try without taking an unnecessary gamble?
  • A review. What would count as evidence for, against, or still uncertain?

Say you're wondering about team leadership. You might ask to lead a short discussion in an existing meeting. Afterwards, look at three kinds of signal:

  • Energy and fit. What interested you, and what drained you? Would you want to do it every week?
  • Capability and learning. What could you already do, and what got better with practice or support?
  • Context and feedback. What did other people notice? And how might their expectations of you have coloured what they said?

Keep other explanations in mind too. Nerves on a first attempt are normal and don't tell you much on their own.

One test rarely settles it. Two or three, each with its own question, usually start to show you something.

Deciding not to is a result too

None of this is meant to talk you into the move. What you're after is a better-informed choice: to carry on, to adapt the plan, to change direction, or to pause.

If you test it properly and it still isn't you, that's a good outcome. You've swapped a vague feeling for something you actually know, and you can stop carrying the question around.
