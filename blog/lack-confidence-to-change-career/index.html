Lack confidence to change career? Check the evidence first

Joe Markland, Career Coach

You've done the thinking already. You know roughly what you'd move into. You've read the job ads, maybe looked at a course, maybe even drafted the first line of a cover letter. Then a quiet voice says you're not ready, and the tab gets closed.

If that sounds familiar, you're in good company. Plenty of capable people stall at exactly this point. They've usually got plenty of information. What's missing is confidence that they could actually pull the move off.

This page is about that specific kind of stuck: you've got the ability, or most of it, but you don't trust it enough to act.

Confidence tends to attach to one task at a time

When people say "I lack confidence", they often mean it as a fixed trait, something you either have or you don't. Albert Bandura's research on self-efficacy points somewhere more useful. The belief that you can carry out an action tends to be specific to that action. You can be completely sure of yourself running a team meeting and wobbly about applying for a job in a new sector.

That matters, because "I'm not a confident person" is very hard to do anything about. "I'm not sure I could make the case for myself in an interview for a project management role" is something you can work with.

So the first useful step is to get specific. What's the action you keep not taking? Applying for the role? Telling your manager? Signing up for the course? Speaking to someone who already does the job? Write it down as one sentence. Vague doubt is much harder to shift than doubt with an address.

The Evidence Discount

Capable people often mark down the evidence of what they can do. The degree counted, but only because the course was easy. The promotion happened, but only because nobody else applied. The positive feedback was just someone being nice.

I call this the Evidence Discount. Each bit of proof gets marked down until it's worth almost nothing, while every doubt is taken at full price. Keep the books like that for long enough and it's no surprise the move feels out of reach.

Telling yourself you're brilliant at everything won't help either. The aim is a fairer look: count your evidence at the price you'd give it if a friend showed you the same CV.

Try this:

  1. Write down the action you're avoiding, in one sentence.
  2. List three things you've done that are relevant to it. Work, volunteering, side projects, anything counts.
  3. Next to each one, write the reason you've been giving yourself for why it doesn't count.
  4. Ask whether you'd accept that reason from a colleague describing their own work.

Some of those reasons will hold up. That's useful to know too.

Real gaps are worth knowing about

Not every doubt is a discount. Sometimes the gap is real: a qualification the role genuinely requires, a skill you've never used, a pay cut you can't absorb. Being honest about those is part of the point.

The difference is that a real gap usually has a next step attached. A course, a conversation, a smaller first role. A discounted strength just has a feeling attached. If you can't name what would close the gap, it's worth checking whether there's a gap there at all.

Waiting for confidence can keep you waiting

A lot of career advice says build your confidence first, then make the move. In practice it often runs the other way round. Courage comes first, and confidence follows from the evidence you collect by doing something.

Nobody's asking you to jump. Pick a step small enough that you'd do it on a Tuesday evening:

  • Message one person who works in the field you're curious about.
  • Apply for one role that's a stretch rather than a leap.
  • Ask your manager about a project in the area you'd like to move towards.

Each of those gives you new evidence, and new evidence is much harder to discount than old evidence you've already talked yourself out of.

A question worth sitting with: where are you demanding certainty from yourself that you wouldn't demand from anyone else?

When it isn't really confidence

Sometimes what feels like low confidence is something else. The risk of the move might feel too big to carry. The new role might make sense on paper but not feel like you yet. Or you might simply be too stretched to take on anything extra right now. Each of those needs a different kind of next step, and pushing harder on confidence won't help if confidence isn't what's in the way.
